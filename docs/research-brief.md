# Research brief: the between-session opportunity in youth basketball training

Prepared October 14, 2026. Four parallel research tracks; sources cited inline.
This is the evidence behind the PROOF thesis (see `README.md`).

---

## 1. THE DISCONNECT — five sharp problem statements

### 1.1 The hour myth: parents buy hours; hours don’t develop players
Deliberate-practice research (Ericsson, 1993) holds that expertise comes from structured
practice “specifically designed to overcome current limitations, monitored by expert
coaches who provide immediate, constructive feedback” — not volume. Macnamara, Hambrick
& Oswald’s 2014 meta-analysis (88 studies) found deliberate practice explains only **12%
of performance variance overall, 18% in sports**; their 2016 sport-specific meta-analysis
found practice explains **~1% of variance among elite performers**. A typical training
client gets 1–2 expert-coached hours out of a 168-hour week. The industry sells the 1–2
hours; the other 166 are unmanaged.

### 1.2 The feedback void: between-session reps happen with zero correction
Motor-learning research shows *targeted* feedback beats constant correction: bandwidth
feedback (correct only outside an agreed range) improved complex skill learning
(Niźnikowski et al., 2025, *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living*); self-controlled
video feedback improved basketball set-shot technique (Aiken, Fairbrother & Post, 2012)
and did the same in 11-year-olds while raising self-efficacy and intrinsic motivation
(Ste-Marie et al., 2013, 2016). McKay et al.’s 2022 meta-analysis (61 papers) found the
coaching-education dogma “less feedback = better learning” is stronger than the evidence.
Most damning: **zero published studies measure between-session practice assignments
(“homework”) in youth sport** — compliance, dosage, design. The layer is so unmanaged
the literature doesn’t study it.

### 1.3 The plateau machine: drills optimize practice performance, not transfer
Classic contextual-interference work (Shea & Morgan, 1979; Porter & Magill’s adolescent
basketball studies) and Guadagnoli & Lee’s Challenge Point Framework (2004): smooth
blocked drills look productive in practice but don’t transfer to games — “performance
during practice does not necessarily represent the permanency and adaptability of skill
learning.” A kid doing the same solo shooting routine between sessions plateaus *by
design*. (Honesty note: a recent meta-analysis titled “The myth of contextual
interference learning benefit in sports practice” finds the effect smaller in real sport
than textbooks suggest — the robust point is the performance-vs-learning distinction,
not “always randomize.”)

### 1.4 The motivation trap: unsupervised work dies without the right climate
Self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan): sustained engagement needs autonomy (choice),
competence (visible progress), relatedness (connection). Controlling, pressuring
approaches predict dropout (Sarrazin et al., 2002; SDT-burnout systematic review). The
coach-athlete relationship (Jowett’s 3Cs) predicts need satisfaction → motivation across
five countries. Gamification: achievement/progress elements (points, streaks, badges)
boost engagement in physical activity — but a 2026 *Scientific Reports* study (n=500)
warned **“public ranking should be used cautiously”**; rewards feed competence until
they become controlling pressure, then they backfire. ~70% of kids drop out of organized
sports by 13 (AAP, 2024 clinical report), driven by pressure and burnout.

### 1.5 The parent black box: the buyer can’t see what they’re buying
Parents spend ~$1,000–2,300/yr on basketball (Aspen Project Play, 2025: $1,016 primary
sport, +46% since 2019; Post et al., 2021: $2,338 median for basketball parents). Cost is
now the **#1 quit driver at 23%**, passing injury (21%) and enjoyment (20%) (Good
Sports/Harris, 2026, n=574). But parents cannot evaluate skill development directly —
they judge on proxies: the kid’s **mood** (does he want to go?), **practice** (does he do
it willingly?), **performance** (did the game go well?) (Dave Simons, retention
consultant). Matt Young (7,000+ youth-sport data points): **“Few service sectors ask
customers to invest so much money and time while accepting so little visibility in
return.”** Retention is “not a moment. It’s a slow, invisible drift” starting month 2–3,
when a parent silently asks *“Is this actually working?”* (Simons). **Caveat: no national
survey isolates “lack of visibility” as a quit reason.** Well-argued by practitioners;
not yet quantified.

## 2. WHAT ACTUALLY WORKS — evidence-backed mechanisms

1. **Targeted, bandwidth-style video feedback — not every-rep correction.** Timestamped
   feedback on a submitted clip sits exactly where the evidence points (Aiken et al., 2012;
   Ste-Marie, 2013/2016; bandwidth-feedback studies).
2. **Assigned work that’s varied and game-like, calibrated to the learner.** Challenge
   Point Framework: optimal difficulty depends on skill level. Load-manage: exceeding a
   2:1 organized-to-free-play ratio predicts injury (Jayanthi, 891 athletes; ACSM).
3. **A three-party proof loop: player submits → coach responds → parent sees.** The
   cleanest mechanic in youth sports isn’t basketball — it’s **MyDojo World** (martial
   arts): kid completes app challenges → parent verifies in companion app (unverified
   doesn’t count) → instructor rewards in class. Basketball proof-of-concept: **I’m
   Possible’s SkillPlus** ($99/mo): named Training Guide, video submission review,
   personalized assignments, weekly check-ins.
4. **SDT-designed motivation: choice, visible progress, relationship.** Assignment choice
   (autonomy), private streaks/progress (competence — never public leaderboards), coach
   relationship carried through the week via feedback (relatedness). Gamified PE explained
   58.9% of perceived learning variance via need satisfaction → motivation → engagement
   (*Scientific Reports*, 2026).
5. **Weekly parent visibility as the retention product.** Parental *support* (not pressure)
   is the strongest predictor of continued participation (β≈.67, Retos, 2025; Gao et al.,
   2024 review); pressure predicts worse performance (B=−0.32). Adjacent proof: tutoring
   company **Tabtor** tiers $30–120/mo pricing partly on **report frequency** — reporting
   itself is a priced feature.
6. **Win the first 90 days.** 26% of new lesson clients never book a second lesson; median
   trainer keeps 24% past 90 days (Baseline Pro, 2026, n=433). Month-to-month members stay
   ~15 months vs. ~36 on 12-month agreements; group routine cuts cancellations ~18%
   (IHRSA).

## 3. MARKET REALITY — how the money flows

### Pricing benchmarks (Oct 2026)
| Offering | Range | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Private 1-on-1, entry | $30–50/hr | Superprof Chicago avg $40/hr |
| Private 1-on-1, established | $60–85/session | Cherry Hill CHHRC $85 / $70 in 4-pack |
| Private 1-on-1, premium | $150–175+/session | Advantage Basketball $150/hr |
| Small-group monthly membership | $119–500/mo | Vault MD $199–449; A1 NJ $300–500; Hoops College NC $240–325 |
| Elite monthly | $500–2,500/mo | Program NYC ($500 youth unlimited; $2,500 Platinum) |
| 3-day camp | $225–295 | Breakthrough Basketball |
| 5-day overnight camp | $995–1,195 | PGC Basketball |
| App-only subscription | $9–99/mo | Techne Futbol $9.99; RealSkill $49; SkillPlus $99 |

**The winning model:** per-session privates cover expenses; **monthly small-group
memberships are the recurring-revenue engine**; camps are seasonal spikes (three-stream
model, operator Danny Cooper, ~$17K/mo example). Growth edge: bundling a between-session
layer into memberships to justify price and cut churn (IPT Wisconsin bundles RealSkill
into $200–400/mo tiers; Program NYC’s $2,500 Platinum bakes in semi-weekly “Vision,
Plan and Accountability” check-ins).

### Who owns what position
- **PGC** — “think the game” via camps ($995–1,195/wk) + “PGC Promise” refund. Accountability
  buried as PGC+ upsell ($230–430).
- **Breakthrough Basketball** — drill libraries/courses ($19–99); sells content, not coaching.
- **CoachUp** — marketplace to *find* a coach ($40–150/hr); no between-session product.
- **HomeCourt / DribbleUp / Ballogy** — self-tracking tools ($7.99/mo; $19.99/mo; free).
  No human coach in the loop.
- **Pure Sweat app** ($4.99/mo) — closest *marketing* to accountability (“the days of
  guessing if you are putting in enough work are over”); no coach involved.
- **I’m Possible SkillPlus** ($99/mo) — closest *mechanics* (Training Guide, video review,
  weekly check-ins) but positioned as remote training, not parent-visible accountability.
- **Program NYC Platinum** ($2,500/mo) — accountability as luxury good.

### The graveyard lesson
Pure remote/app without a human coach doesn’t sustain: HomeCourt raised **$8.5M**
(Cuban, Nash, Tsai) then **pivoted to entertainment**; Wilson X connected ball dormant;
DribbleUp ~30% 1–2-star reviews on tracking failures. **The human coach in the loop is
the durable part** — good news for an in-person trainer.

## 4. THE OPENING — the gap nobody owns

**“We make sure your kid actually does the work — and you see it every week.”**

No brand owns this as its *lead* promise. PGC sells camp weeks. Breakthrough sells drill
libraries. CoachUp sells discovery. Apps sell self-tracking with no coach watching. The
two closest owners bury it (PGC’s accountability is an upsell two clicks deep; SkillPlus
is positioned as remote training; Program NYC prices it at $2,500/mo).

**Why a small in-person trainer can claim it:** the loop requires ongoing human labor
(assign, review proof, report) that national brands can’t scale cheaply — so they automate
it or luxury-price it. A local trainer with 20–40 kids *can* do the human loop; the labor
intensity is the moat. The buyer’s two unanswered questions — “does my kid do the work?”
and “is this worth $X a month?” — every major brand answers neither.

## 5. HONESTY SUMMARY — solid vs. thin

- **Solid:** practice-volume limits (Macnamara meta-analyses); feedback-quality evidence
  (bandwidth, video); SDT → persistence/dropout; parental support → continuation (β≈.67);
  spending levels (Aspen, Post et al.); churn ranking, cost #1 (Good Sports/Harris 2026);
  pricing benchmarks (dozens of named businesses); competitor positioning (public homepage
  copy, Oct 2026).
- **Thin:** willingness to pay *specifically* for visibility/accountability — no direct
  survey exists; the case rests on Tabtor’s priced reporting tiers and venture bets
  (Shoot 360’s $7M raise, TeachMe.To’s $2M). The black box as a churn *mechanism* is
  well-argued but unquantified. The *specific design* (feedback type, dosage,
  gamification mix) is unproven — the evidence supports the direction, not the recipe.
- **Inferred (the product hypothesis):** that parent visibility into between-session work
  drives adherence and retention. Support → adherence is established; *visibility as the
  lever* is what a pilot would test.
